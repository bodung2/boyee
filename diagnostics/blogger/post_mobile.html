Korean School Grades Explained: How Foreign Children Are Placed in 2026

Korean school grade placement follows a March school year, but foreign children are placed by records and completed schooling, not age alone.

That distinction matters when a family moves from a country whose school year begins in August or September. A birth-year chart can give you a useful first estimate, but the receiving Korean school may also examine transcripts, attendance periods, and the curriculum already completed. The safest approach is to treat age as a starting point and the school’s placement decision as the answer.

How is the Korean school system organized?

Korea uses a 6-3-3 structure before university: six years of elementary school, three years of middle school, and three years of high school. The Ministry of Education says the first semester begins in March and the second begins near the end of August. Summer vacation usually falls in July and August, while the long winter break falls between December and February.

Elementary and middle school are the compulsory stages. The ordinary path therefore runs from elementary Grade 1 through Grade 6, middle school Years 1 through 3, and high school Years 1 through 3. English speakers should be careful with the last two labels: a Korean student in middle school Year 1 has completed six elementary grades, so the rough US-style equivalent is Grade 7.

  • Elementary school: 6 years
  • Middle school: 3 years
  • High school: 3 years
  • Academic year: March through the following February

This March calendar is the first source of confusion for families arriving from an August-to-June system. A child can appear to be between grades even when both systems are working normally.

Which birth year normally enters Grade 1 in 2026?

Under Article 13 of Korea’s Elementary and Secondary Education Act, the standard rule is entry on March 1 of the year after the calendar year in which a child turns six. That means children born in 2019 are the ordinary Grade 1 cohort beginning in March 2026.

This does not mean every new first grader is exactly six on opening day. A child born in January 2019 is already seven by March 1, 2026, while a child born in December 2019 is still six. Korea groups the normal intake by calendar year of birth, not by a September cutoff.

The same law permits early or delayed entry within a limited range. A child may enter in the year after turning five or in the year after turning seven. For a 2026 start, that makes 2020 and 2018 possible birth years in exceptional early or delayed cases. Those are options, not the default cohort.

The legal age rule is framed as a schooling obligation for Korean citizens. Foreign children can attend Korean schools, but families should not assume that the standard admission notice will arrive automatically. Local government guidance notes that a foreign child who does not receive a notice may need a parent or guardian to apply directly.

How is a foreign child’s grade actually decided?

For a child transferring from abroad, the practical question is not simply, “How old are they?” The school looks at prior education. Local Korean guidance describes placement based on the period of attendance and the curriculum shown on school records, translated into Korea’s school structure.

This becomes important when academic calendars overlap or leave a gap. Dongjak District’s English-language guide gives a concrete rule of thumb: if a semester overlaps because the foreign school started in September, the Korean placement calculation may move the student forward by one semester; if a semester is missing, it may move the student back by one semester. The final decision depends on the records and the responsible school or education authority.

A practical first estimate for the regular 2026 Korean cohorts is:

  • Born in 2019: elementary Grade 1
  • Born in 2018: elementary Grade 2
  • Born in 2013: middle school Year 1
  • Born in 2010: high school Year 1

These examples assume uninterrupted schooling on the ordinary Korean timetable. They are not guaranteed placements for a transfer student. A student’s transcript, completed subjects, previous school calendar, and any gaps or acceleration can change the result.

Midyear moves deserve extra care. An American student finishing one grade in June may arrive after Korea has already completed much of its first semester. Older high school students face an additional problem: transferred credits, required subjects, and graduation timing may not line up neatly. Families of students near graduation should ask for a written explanation of credit recognition before committing to a school.

Where do you apply, and what documents should you bring?

For elementary school, local guidance directs families to a school serving their place of residence. The principal reviews admission after residence and immigration information are checked. Middle-school assignment is generally handled through the local education support office, while high-school admission or transfer follows the relevant education office and school rules.

Exact paperwork varies, so confirm the list before visiting. Official local guides commonly identify the following:

  • Passport or other identity document
  • Alien registration certificate or proof of domestic residence, when applicable
  • Certificate of entry and exit
  • Certificate of enrollment or graduation from the previous school
  • Academic transcripts showing grades and attendance periods
  • Korean translations and authentication where requested
  • Proof of the family’s Korean address
  • Vaccination or health records when requested

Bring more detail than a single report card if you can. Records that state the school calendar, dates attended, grade completed, and subjects studied help the Korean school distinguish a genuine gap from a calendar mismatch. Requirements can differ by region and student status, so contact the school and the local education office rather than relying only on an online checklist.

Can foreign children attend a regular Korean public school?

Yes. Seoul Metropolitan Government states that foreign children can generally attend Korean schools, and other local government guidance explains how foreign residents apply. Regular public school and a foreign or international school are different paths: the former follows Korea’s national system and teaches mainly in Korean, while the latter may use another curriculum and has its own eligibility, fees, and placement rules.

Korean ability is not merely an admissions detail. It affects whether a child can follow explanations, complete assignments, and build friendships. Seoul’s guidance advises families to consult the school when a child lacks Korean proficiency. Ask what Korean-language support is actually available, how often it is provided, and whether the child will spend part of the day outside the regular classroom.

Do not assume every school offers the same support. Programs and staffing vary. The 2022 revised national curriculum includes a Korean-language curriculum, but the form of local support still depends on the education office and school. If language assistance is central to the decision, confirm the program before signing a housing contract.

Public elementary tuition is generally free, but families may still encounter costs connected with supplies, activities, care, or meals depending on the program and locality. A city guide is useful for orientation, but the school’s current written information is the better source for the actual amount.

What do foreign parents most often miss?

The biggest mistake is treating grade conversion as arithmetic. “Age seven equals Grade 1” is too vague because it hides the birth-month issue, the March start, early or delayed entry, and the child’s completed schooling.

  1. Start with the birth year. This identifies the ordinary Korean cohort.
  2. Map completed semesters. Compare actual attendance dates, not just grade names.
  3. Check for overlap or gaps. An August or September school year rarely aligns perfectly with Korea’s March calendar.
  4. Ask who makes the decision. Elementary, middle, and high-school procedures are not identical.
  5. Verify language and learning support. Placement on paper is only one part of a successful transition.

Families also sometimes confuse the public-school route with admission to a foreign school. Foreign schools have separate eligibility and admissions rules; Korean students may need qualifying overseas residence or another statutory basis. If a school markets itself in English, that alone does not tell you its legal category, curriculum, accreditation, or whether its credentials fit your child’s future plans.

If you are arranging the move itself, Korea’s road-name and lot-number address system is worth understanding because residence helps determine the local school contact. Parents comparing daily routines may also find the guide to Korean school lunch useful.

Bottom line

For 2026, a child born in 2019 ordinarily starts Korean elementary Grade 1, but a foreign transfer student’s final placement may reflect prior schooling and calendar differences. Contact the school or education office early, carry detailed records, and ask for confirmation of both grade and support arrangements.

As a teacher and a parent, I think the best placement is not necessarily the highest grade a document can justify. In my view, the right choice is the one that gives the child a realistic path to learn, adjust, and grow without turning one calendar mismatch into a lasting burden.
